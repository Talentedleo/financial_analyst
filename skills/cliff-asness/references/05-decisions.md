# 05-decisions.md — Cliff Asness's Key Investment Decisions

> Major decisions in Asness's career, from academia to AQR.

---

## 1. From Academia to Goldman Sachs (1991-1994)

**Context:** Asness was a PhD student at Chicago, originally planning to be a professor.

**The Decision:** Took a summer job at Goldman Sachs Asset Management in 1991, then stayed on. Eventually turned down a job offer from PIMCO to stay at Goldman and build their quant group.

**The Logic:**
- Saw the opportunity to apply academic research to real-world investing
- "I can pursue the academic stuff that I really have grown to love and I can see if it works in the real world"
- Financial incentive: "someone who says they chose Goldman Sachs over being an academic and doesn't admit that was part of the equation is probably not telling the full truth"

**The Outcome:**
- Built Goldman Sachs's quant research team from scratch
- Hired John Liew, Ross Stevens, and Brian Hurst from Chicago
- Launched the Global Alpha fund, one of the first systematic hedge funds
- Global Alpha grew to $12B AUM at its peak

**Fama's Reaction:** "Why do you wanna stay and be a salesman?"

---

## 2. Founding AQR Capital Management (1998)

**Context:** Asness was running Goldman's successful quant group. The LTCM crisis of 1998 was about to unfold.

**The Decision:** Left Goldman Sachs with three co-founders (David Kabiller, John Liew, Robert Krail) to start AQR Capital Management.

**The Logic:**
- Wanted independence to apply factor investing at scale
- Goldman's structure limited the scope of what they could build
- AQR was founded as an SEC-registered firm from day one — unusual for hedge funds in 1998

**The Outcome:**
- AQR launched with approximately $1 billion in initial capital
- One of the largest fund launches at the time
- Survived the LTCM crisis and 2008 financial crisis
- Grew to over $185B AUM at peak

**Key Quote:** "It was the perfect time to start a quant firm — right before the worst period for quants in history."

---

## 3. Surviving the 2008 Financial Crisis

**Context:** AQR's assets declined from $39B in 2007 to $17B by end of 2008. The quant industry was devastated.

**The Decision:** Stayed the course. Did not abandon factor-based strategies despite massive drawdowns and client redemptions.

**The Logic:**
- Factors were still working in the long-term data; the crisis was a tail event
- "If you believe in your factors during good times, you have to believe in them during bad times"
- Maintained disciplined exposures rather than panic-reducing risk

**The Outcome:**
- AQR recovered strongly post-2008
- By 2010, AUM had bounced back to $33B
- The firm's commitment to its process during the crisis built long-term credibility

---

## 4. Launching Alternative Mutual Funds (2009)

**Context:** Post-crisis, investors were demanding more liquid, transparent alternatives to hedge funds.

**The Decision:** AQR became one of the first investment managers to offer a family of alternative mutual funds (liquid alts).

**The Logic:**
- Democratizing access to factor-based strategies
- Lower fees, daily liquidity, and regulatory transparency
- Hedge funds were facing a reputational crisis; liquid alts filled a market need

**The Outcome:**
- AQR's liquid alternative funds became "category leaders" in Morningstar's 2015 rankings
- In 2016, Bloomberg reported: "Cliff Asness's Alternative Funds Top Rivals With 17% Returns"
- By 2017, Forbes noted Asness had "moved away from hedge funds" toward lower-fee products

---

## 5. The Factor-Timing Debate (2010s)

**Context:** Many practitioners argued that investors should time factors — adding value when it's cheap, reducing momentum when it's expensive.

**The Decision:** Asness argued that factor timing is extremely difficult and that static multi-factor allocation is superior.

**The Logic:**
- "Market timing: Sin a little" — resolving the debate on whether investors can time factors
- The evidence showed that most timing strategies fail to outperform a simple static allocation
- The costs and complexity of timing often outweigh the benefits

**The Outcome:**
- AQR maintained its static multi-factor approach
- Many timing-focused competitors underperformed
- Asness's position became the industry standard for serious factor investors

---

## 6. Publishing "Quality Minus Junk" (2014)

**Context:** The quality factor was not well-defined in academic literature. Practitioners had intuitive notions of quality but no rigorous framework.

**The Decision:** Asness and co-authors developed the most comprehensive quality factor definition to date — combining profitability, growth, safety, and payout.

**The Logic:**
- Quality needed a rigorous, quantitative definition — not "I know it when I see it"
- Quality is negatively correlated with value, making it an excellent portfolio diversifier
- Quality stocks offer higher returns with lower risk — a true anomaly

**The Outcome:**
- "Quality Minus Junk" (QMJ) became a standard factor in both academic research and industry practice
- Won the Bernstein Fabozzi/Jacobs Levy Award
- AQR integrated QMJ into its flagship strategies

---

## 7. The "Less-Efficient Market Hypothesis" (2024)

**Context:** The rise of passive investing, ETFs, zero-day options, and retail trading was transforming market structure.

**The Decision:** Asness published a paper arguing that markets are becoming LESS efficient, not more.

**The Logic:**
- More participants chasing short-term signals creates more inefficiencies
- Systematic active managers have more, not fewer, opportunities
- The proliferation of passive investing disconnects prices from fundamentals

**The Outcome:**
- Became one of Asness's most discussed papers
- Generated significant industry debate
- Positioned AQR as the intellectual leader arguing for active systematic management

---

## 8. Investing in Niche Plus / ClearAlpha Technologies (2023)

**Context:** The multi-manager hedge fund model was attracting massive capital.

**The Decision:** Asness and other institutional investors committed several hundred million dollars to Niche Plus, a multi-manager fund focused on niche strategies including temperature arbitrage.

**The Logic:**
- Niche strategies offer uncorrelated returns that complement AQR's factor-based approach
- Co-run by AQR alumnus Brian Hurst
- Diversification into alternative return streams

**The Outcome:**
- Early stage; results still developing
- Demonstrates Asness's willingness to invest in new models

---

## Key Decision Patterns

1. **Conviction in data:** Every major decision is anchored in empirical evidence
2. **Long-term orientation:** Short-term pain is tolerated for long-term gain
3. **Intellectual honesty:** Admits mistakes publicly (the value drought, 2008, etc.)
4. **Scale matters:** Builds strategies that can absorb large amounts of capital
5. **Academic rigor:** Decisions are research-driven, not instinct-driven
