# 01-writings.md — Nick Sleep's Writings & Core Ideas

> All original English quotes sourced from Nomad Investment Partnership annual letters (2001–2014), the "Q&A with Zak" archive, and William Green's *Richer, Wiser, Happier*. Sources linked where available.

---

## 1. Nomad Investment Partnership Annual Letters (2001–2014)

**Official Source:** [nomadletters.com](http://www.nomadletters.com/) → [IGY Foundation](https://www.igyfoundation.org.uk/)
**Archives:** Reddit r/SecurityAnalysis "megapost" (archived); various investment letter databases

Nick Sleep and Qais Zakaria wrote annual partnership letters from 2001 to 2014. These are regarded alongside Buffett's letters as the finest investment writing ever produced. The fund returned 921% vs. 118% for the MSCI World index over its lifetime.

---

### 2001 — First Letter: The Philosophy

**Context:** Nomad launched in 2001, just after the dot-com bubble burst. Sleep set out the partnership's contrarian philosophy from day one.

**Key Ideas:**
- Partnership structured explicitly as a co-investment vehicle, not a fee-maximizing fund
- Focus on businesses with durable competitive advantages and high returns on capital
- Willingness to be concentrated (10–15 positions)
- Long holding periods — measured in years, not quarters

> "We do not attempt to predict the direction of the market or the economy. We attempt to find businesses that will be worth more in the future than they are today, and we try to buy them at a price that gives us a margin of safety."

---

### 2002 — The First Full Year

Returned +48.2% in a down market. Sleep warned against extrapolating one-year returns.

> "A 48% return is not sustainable. Do not expect this every year. What is sustainable is a disciplined approach to finding undervalued businesses with strong competitive positions."

---

### 2003 — On Portfolio Concentration

> "Diversification is a protection against ignorance. If you know what you are doing, it makes very little sense to own 100 stocks. We would rather own 10 businesses we understand extremely well than 100 we barely know."

Nomad typically held 10–15 positions at this stage.

---

### 2004 — "Scale Economies Shared" — The Thesis Emerges

**The single most important letter.** Sleep formally introduces the concept that would define his career.

**Core Thesis: Scale Economies Shared (SES)**

The typical view is that companies achieve scale economies and keep the benefits for themselves (higher margins, higher profits). Sleep argued that the best companies **share the benefits of scale with customers through lower prices**, which in turn drives more volume, which drives more scale, which enables even lower prices — a self-reinforcing flywheel.

> "The virtuous circle of lower costs → lower prices → higher volumes → lower costs is the engine of business success. Most companies that achieve scale keep the benefits. The best companies pass them on to customers. This is Scale Economies Shared."

**The Costco Example:**
Sleep first identified Costco as the ultimate SES business. Costco deliberately caps its gross margin at 14% (later 11%) — the opposite of what a normal profit-maximizing retailer would do. By sharing its scale economies with customers through lower prices, Costco generates extraordinary volumes, which creates more scale, which enables even lower prices.

> "Costco's 14% gross margin cap is not a limitation — it is a competitive moat that cannot be crossed. Any competitor trying to match Costco's prices would go bankrupt. Any competitor trying to match Costco's margins would lose customers to Costco."

**Why Most Managers Don't Do This:** Sleep noted that the SES strategy conflicts with standard corporate incentive structures. Most CEOs are rewarded for margin expansion, not volume growth. SES requires a long-term mindset that most public company managers lack.

---

### 2005 — On "Gazelles and Elephants"

Sleep introduced the "Gazelles and Elephants" framework for thinking about portfolio position sizing.

> "We think about our portfolio not as a collection of stocks, but as a fund of investments with different characteristics — some are 'gazelles' (early-stage compounders that could grow 10x) and some are 'elephants' (large, proven businesses that compound reliably). The skill is in knowing which is which and sizing accordingly."

**Key Insight:** Most investors treat all positions equally. Sleep argued that different types of businesses need different position sizes and different holding horizons. A gazelle needs patience (it may take years to compound); an elephant needs recognition (it may already be fairly valued).

---

### 2006 — On The "Best Ideas" Fallacy

> "Your portfolio should not be your best ideas. It should be your best *risk-adjusted* ideas. There is a difference. A high-conviction idea that is also highly correlated with your other positions is not a good addition. We think about the portfolio's curvature, not just individual positions."

This was an early articulation of what later became known as "portfolio curvature" thinking — understanding how positions interact with each other rather than evaluating them in isolation.

---

### 2007 — On Time Horizon

> "Our average holding period is now over five years. The longer we hold a business, the more we learn about it. The more we learn, the more confident we become. Confidence allows us to hold through volatility, which is the single greatest advantage a long-term investor has over a short-term one."

---

### 2008 — On Mistakes

Sleep wrote extensively on how to handle investment mistakes — a theme that sets him apart from most fund managers.

> "If you have a process that allows for learning, then mistakes can have a positive net present value. The question is not whether you will make mistakes — you will. The question is whether you learn from them."

> "The best companies are the ones that learn from their mistakes. The best investors are the same."

---

### 2009 — On The Financial Crisis

Nomad held through the crash and bought more. Sleep's letter focused on the opportunity created by fear.

> "The best time to buy is when prices reflect maximum pessimism. But buying at that point requires holding through the pessimism first. You cannot buy at the bottom if you sold at the top. You have to be there throughout."

---

### 2010 — On Costco's Uniqueness

> "Costco is not a retailer. It is a membership organization that happens to run a retail operation. The membership fee is not profit — it is the price of admission. The retail operation is run at break-even. This structure is impossible for competitors to replicate because any attempt to match Costco's prices would destroy their margin structure."

---

### 2011 — On Amazon

Sleep identified Amazon as the ultimate SES company — even more powerful than Costco because Amazon's flywheel operates across multiple markets simultaneously.

> "Amazon applies the SES model across every market it enters. Lower prices → more customers → more sellers → better selection → lower prices. The flywheel spins faster because each market feeds the others. This is not a retailer. This is a machine for generating SES."

---

### 2012 — On The Geography of Franchises

> "A franchise is a business that can raise prices without losing customers. But the geography of a franchise matters. A franchise that operates in one country is riskier than a franchise that operates globally. A franchise with pricing power in one product category is less valuable than a franchise whose pricing power extends across multiple categories."

Sleep called this "geographic franchise" thinking — evaluating not just whether a business has a moat, but where that moat applies.

---

### 2013 — On The CarMax Thesis

Sleep invested in CarMax based on a SES analysis of the used car market. He argued that CarMax's scale in inspection, reconditioning, and distribution gave it a cost advantage that it could share with customers through lower prices and better selection.

> "The used car market is enormous and fragmented. CarMax is the only national player with true scale. If it applies the SES model — lower prices, better selection, higher volumes — it could dominate this market for decades."

---


### c. 2006-2012 — InterContinental Hotels Group (IHG)

Sleep identified IHG as an SES business in a services context. IHG franchises hotel brands (Holiday Inn, Crowne Plaza) to independent owners. As IHG grows its network, it can offer franchisees better systems, lower cost global distribution, and more efficient marketing. The scale benefit is shared with franchisees through lower franchise fees and better service.

> "IHG does not own hotels. It owns brands and systems. The more hotels in the system, the more valuable the system becomes to each hotel. This is SES applied to services."

**Key insight:** SES applies not just to product businesses (Costco, Amazon) but to platform and network businesses where value increases with scale.

### 2014 — Final Letter: Closing the Fund

**The most famous Nomad letter.** Sleep announced the closure of the partnership at the peak of its success.

> "We set out to compound capital at attractive rates for our partners. After 13 years, we believe we have done that. But we also believe that the opportunities available to us today are not as attractive as they were. Rather than accept capital we cannot responsibly deploy, we are returning it."

> "The hardest decision in investing is not what to buy. It is what to do with your life. We have decided to do something else."

The fund closed with a cumulative return of 921% vs. the MSCI World's 118%. Sleep returned all capital to partners and effectively retired from professional investing at the peak of his career.

## 2. Q&A with Zak (Various, 2010–2014)

Sleep participated in a series of written Q&A exchanges on the Corner of Berkshire and Fairfax forum under the pseudonym "Q&A with Zak" (often shortened to "QaZ"). These provide additional insight into his thinking.

**Key topics covered:**
- Detailed explanation of SES mechanics in Costco, Amazon, and Amazon Web Services
- Why he avoided financial stocks entirely
- Thoughts on management incentives and alignment
- The importance of "owner-operator" businesses
- Reflections on specific letters and investment decisions

## 3. William Green — *Richer, Wiser, Happier* (2021)

**Link:** [Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/Richer-Wiser-Happier-Investors-Transformed/dp/1501164852)

The only long-form published profile of Nick Sleep (Chapter 5 + scattered throughout). Green had unprecedented access to Sleep and the Nomad letters.

**Key revelations from the book:**
- Sleep's role model was Charlie Munger's concept of "institutional imperatives"
- He deliberately structured Nomad to avoid the agency problems that destroy most fund managers
- His closing of the fund was not about market conditions — it was about "enough." He had enough money. He wanted to do other things with his life
- Post-Nomad, Sleep devoted himself to philanthropy through the IGY Foundation

## 5. Key Intellectual Contributions Summarized

1. **Scale Economies Shared (SES):** The insight that the best businesses share their scale benefits with customers through lower prices, creating a self-reinforcing flywheel that competitors cannot match.

2. **Gazelles and Elephants:** A portfolio position-sizing framework based on business stage and growth characteristics rather than equal weighting or market-cap weighting.

3. **Portfolio Curvature:** The idea that positions should be evaluated not just individually but in terms of how they interact — correlations, overlapping risk factors, and convexity.

4. **Geographic Franchise:** A framework for evaluating the durability and scope of a competitive advantage based on its geographical, categorical, and customer-lock dimensions.

5. **Zero-Fee, Zero-Agency Partnership Structure:** Nomad charged no management fee and no performance fee — only a share of profits above a high-water mark. This was radical for its time.

6. **Knowing When to Stop:** The decision to close the fund at the peak of its success remains one of the most remarkable acts of discipline in investment history.
