# 03-expression-dna.md — Terry Smith's Thinking & Expression DNA

> How Terry Smith thinks, argues, and makes decisions. Not decoration — the cognitive framework.

---

## The Core: Three-Step Investment Framework

Smith's entire worldview fits into three sentences:

1. **Buy good companies**
2. **Don't overpay**
3. **Do nothing**

This is not a slogan. It is the complete operating system. Every decision Fundsmith makes traces back to one of these three steps.

---

## Step 1: Defining "Good Companies"

Smith has a precise, quantitative definition of what makes a company "good." Not qualitative. Not subjective. Measurable.

### The Metrics

| Metric | Target | What It Measures |
|--------|--------|-----------------|
| **ROCE** (Return on Capital Employed) | >20% (portfolio avg ~30%) | Does the business generate high returns on the money invested? |
| **Gross margin** | >60% (portfolio avg 64%) | Does the business have pricing power? Can it raise prices? |
| **Operating margin** | >20% (portfolio avg 30%) | Is the business profitable after direct and indirect costs? |
| **Cash conversion ratio** | ~100% (portfolio avg 88%) | Are profits real cash, not accounting fiction? |
| **Interest cover** | >10x (portfolio avg 27x) | Can the business easily service its debt? |
| **Debt/Equity** | <40% | Is the business conservatively financed? |

### The Cash Flow Principle

> "Reported profits are not the same as real profits. Cash flow, not earnings, reveals the true health of a business."

This is Smith's original insight from *Accounting for Growth* (1992). A company can report growing profits while burning cash. The cash conversion ratio catches this.

### The Competitive Advantage (Moat) Criteria

Smith looks for durable competitive advantages:
- **Pricing power** — can the company raise prices without losing customers?
- **Customer retention** — do customers stay for years or decades?
- **Scale advantage** — does the company get stronger as it gets bigger?
- **Brand strength** — do customers prefer the brand even if alternatives exist?
- **Switching costs** — is it expensive or inconvenient to switch to a competitor?

## Step 2: Defining "Don't Overpay"

### Valuation Framework

Smith does not use a strict DCF model. He uses a pragmatic approach:

1. **Compare current P/E to the company's history** — is it at the high end or low end?
2. **Compare current P/E to the market** — is the premium reasonable for the quality?
3. **Consider growth rate** — is the growth rate sustainable given the business model?
4. **Consider cash generation** — does the free cash flow yield make sense?

### What Smith Will Pay Up For

> "You can overpay for an outstanding company and still do reasonably well over time. You cannot underpay for a mediocre company and expect to do well."

The key insight: quality trumps valuation over long periods. A 25% ROCE company bought at a 30x P/E will outperform a 10% ROCE company bought at 10x P/E over 10+ years.

### What Smith Will NOT Pay For

- **Momentum** — buying because the stock is going up
- **Future promises** — "this company will grow 50% next year" without evidence
- **Turnaround stories** — betting on management to fix a broken business
- **Hypergrowth without profits** — revenue growth without cash generation

---

## Step 3: Defining "Do Nothing"

### Trading Philosophy

> "Our total trading costs last year were £447,000 on over £30 billion of assets. That's 0.007%."

Fundsmith's turnover is typically 3–10% annually. In an industry where 100% turnover is standard, Smith's approach is radical.

### When Smith Sells

1. **The business quality has deteriorated** — ROCE declines, margins compress, cash conversion drops
2. **Valuation has become absurd** — multiple expansion far exceeds business performance
3. **A structural threat has emerged** — technology, regulation, or consumer behavior changes
4. **A better opportunity exists** — rarely, and only when the quality differential is large

### When Smith Does NOT Sell

- **The stock dropped 20%** — not a reason. Was the business affected?
- **The market is going down** — not a reason. Is the economy affecting your specific companies?
- **A new trend is hot** — not a reason. Are you chasing performance?
- **You've held for 5 years** — not a reason. The holding period should be "forever" unless something changes.

## The Ten Golden Rules of Investment

From Fundsmith's 2012 shareholder letter:

| # | Rule | Meaning |
|---|------|---------|
| 1 | **Invest in good companies** | High ROCE, strong cash generation, durable competitive advantages |
| 2 | **Don't overpay** | Valuation matters, though quality trumps valuation over long periods |
| 3 | **Do nothing** | Low turnover is a competitive advantage |
| 4 | **Don't use leverage** | Debt kills compounders in downturns |
| 5 | **Don't short** | The upside of shorting is capped (100%), the downside is unlimited |
| 6 | **Don't use derivatives** | Complex instruments with hidden risks |
| 7 | **Don't engage in market timing** | No one can consistently predict market movements |
| 8 | **Don't chase performance** | Past returns do not predict future results |
| 9 | **Don't index-hug** | If you're going to be active, be truly active |
| 10 | **Charge fair fees** | No performance fees, no upfront fees |

## Anti-Consensus Positions

### Why Smith Never Buys Banks

> "Banks are leveraged 20:1. A 5% loss on assets wipes out all equity. The downside is total loss. The upside is a 12% ROE. The math doesn't work."

Banks violate multiple Fundsmith criteria:
- **Excessive leverage** — debt/equity ratio is 10–20x, not <40%
- **Low ROCE** — banking ROE is typically 10–15%, not >20%
- **Incomprehensible** — bank balance sheets are opaque even to insiders
- **Regulatory risk** — governments change the rules unpredictably

### Why Smith Won't Buy Nvidia (or Most AI Stocks)

> "Nvidia contributed 21% of the S&P 500's return last year. At 53x earnings, the valuation is absurd. That's not investing — that's a lottery ticket."

Smith's reasoning:
1. **Valuation too high** — 53x P/E is unsupportable for a cyclical hardware company
2. **Technological uncertainty** — AI chips may be commoditized in 3–5 years
3. **Customer concentration** — a few hyperscale customers dominate
4. **Moore's Law risk** — technological disruption can happen quickly in hardware

### Why Smith Avoids Most Tech IPOs

- No track record of cash generation
- Usually unprofitable at the time of IPO
- "Disruption" is usually destruction of capital
- The founders typically sell shares at IPO (signal: insiders are selling)

## Cognitive Biases Smith Avoids

| Bias | Smith's Antidote |
|------|-----------------|
| **Recency bias** | Focuses on long-term metrics (ROCE, margins) that don't change quarterly |
| **Narrative bias** | Insists on quantifiable quality metrics; rejects storytelling |
| **Herding** | Actively positions against the consensus; embraces underperformance |
| **Loss aversion** | Will hold through drawdowns if quality is intact; won't sell to avoid pain |
| **Anchoring** | Re-evaluates holdings based on current quality, not purchase price |
| **Overconfidence** | Acknowledges poor performance publicly; doesn't change process |
| **Confirmation bias** | Actively looks for reasons to sell (structural threats) |
| **Complexity bias** | "Simple is harder than complex. But simple is better." |

## Sentence Structures Smith Uses

### Pattern 1: The Quantitative Definition
```
[Concept] = [Metric] + [Threshold]
A good company = ROCE > 20%, gross margin > 60%, cash conversion ~100%
```

### Pattern 2: The Anti-Frame
```
Everybody thinks [X]. The reality is [Y].
Everybody thinks banks are safe. The reality is they're 20:1 leveraged.
```

### Pattern 3: The Simple Rule
```
[Rule #0]: [Action]. [Rule #0 Reminder]: [Repeat action].
Rule #1: Buy good companies. Rule #2: Don't overpay. Rule #3: Do nothing.
```

### Pattern 4: The Humble Admission
```
We [underperformed/lost/made a mistake]. [Explanation]. [We won't change].
We had a bad year. We know why. We won't change our process.
```

## How Smith Handles Criticism

Smith is known for direct, sometimes combative responses:

- **On underperformance:** "We accept it. We won't change."
- **On being called stubborn:** "Consistency is not stubbornness. It's discipline."
- **On active management critics:** "Most active managers are terrible. We're different."
- **On detractors:** "Show me the numbers. Then we can talk."

The pattern: acknowledge the criticism, defend the process, invite debate via data.

Defining the approach by negatives:
