# 05-decisions.md — Terry Smith's Key Decisions

> The critical moments that defined Smith's career. Each decision is documented with context, reasoning, and outcome.

---

## Decision 1: Writing *Accounting for Growth* — and Getting Fired (1992)

**Context:** Smith was a top-ranked banking analyst at UBS Phillips & Drew. He wrote a book exposing 12 accounting techniques that companies used to inflate reported profits.

**The Conflict:**
> "They told me I couldn't publish the book. I said I was going to anyway. They fired me."

**Why He Published Anyway:**
- The book was factual and well-researched
- Smith believed the information was in the public interest
- Several FTSE 100 companies had gone bust despite reporting "healthy" profits (Polly Peck, British & Commonwealth)
- He had integrity-based conviction

**Outcome:**
- Book topped UK bestseller charts, displaced Stephen Hawking's *A Brief History of Time* from #1
- Sold over 100,000 copies
- Smith was fired from UBS
- The book established his reputation as a rigorous independent analyst
- It also made him unemployable at large financial institutions for years

**Lesson:** Conviction in the face of institutional pressure. Smith chose truth over career.

---

## Decision 2: Joining Collins Stewart as CEO (1997)

**Context:** After being fired from UBS, Smith spent years in a smaller, more entrepreneurial environment. In 1997, he joined Collins Stewart, a small stockbroker.

**Why He Joined:**
- Small enough that he could make decisions independently
- No big bank bureaucracy
- Opportunity to build something from a lower base

**Outcome:**
- Built Collins Stewart into a significant mid-cap investment bank
- Led the merger with Tullett Prebon in 2006
- Became CEO of Tullett Prebon, a FTSE 250 interdealer broker
- Retired (briefly) in 2009 with significant personal wealth

---

## Decision 3: Not Writing Another Book After *Accounting for Growth*

**Context:** Smith wrote *Accounting for Growth* in 1992 — and then did not write another book for 28 years.

**Why:**
- Didn't want to be "the accounting guy" forever
- Felt he hadn't accumulated enough new insights worth a book
- *Investing for Growth* (2020) was an anthology of existing writing, not a new thesis

**Outcome:** *Investing for Growth* (2020, 2nd ed. 2023) is an anthology of Fundsmith letters, FT columns, and blog posts.

---

## Decision 4: Founding Fundsmith at Age 57 (2010)

**Context:** After retiring (briefly) from Tullett Prebon, Smith was bored. He had the money, the reputation, but no outlet for his investing passion.

**Why He Started:**
- "I missed investing. I missed the puzzle."
- The active management industry was charging high fees for poor performance
- Smith believed he could demonstrate that disciplined, low-cost, high-quality investing works
- He had an existing following from *Accounting for Growth* and his FT columns

**The Challenge:**
- Starting a fund management business at 57 is unusual
- The industry is relationship-driven; Smith was known as an analyst and broker, not a fund manager
- He needed to raise assets to be viable

**Outcome:**
- Launched in November 2010 with initial assets under management
- By 2025, AUM exceeded £30 billion
- Fundsmith became one of the most successful UK-domiciled funds of all time

**Lesson:** It's never too late to start. Skills and reputation compound across careers.

---

## Decision 5: No Performance Fee, No Upfront Fee (2010)

**Context:** When Fundsmith launched, the standard industry fee model was "2 and 20" — 2% annual management charge plus 20% of outperformance.

**Smith's Decision:**
- Annual management charge: ~0.9% (now ~0.85% at largest tiers)
- Performance fee: 0% (zero)
- Upfront/entry fee: 0% (zero)
- Exit fee: 0% (zero)

**Why:**
> "The industry average is 1.5% plus 20% of everything above a benchmark. That's theft dressed up as performance."

**Impact on Industry:**
Fundsmith's fee structure put pressure on competitors to reduce fees. Many UK fund groups subsequently reduced charges. The "2 and 20" model is now rare in UK retail funds.

---

## Decision 6: The "Our Promise" Framework (2010)

**Context:** At launch, Smith published a list of things Fundsmith would NOT do.

**The Promise:**

| Promise | Why |
|---------|-----|
| No performance fees | Aligns interests |
| No upfront fees | No hidden costs |
| No nonsense | Straight-talking communication |
| No debt | Avoids leverage risk |
| No derivatives | Avoids hidden risk |
| No shorting | Avoids unlimited downside |
| No market timing | Nobody can do it |
| No index hugging | Be truly active or not at all |
| No trading | Low turnover saves costs |
| No hedging | Hedging is expensive |

**Why This Matters:**
Most fund managers define themselves by what they do. Smith defined Fundsmith by what it doesn't do. This created a clear identity and made the fund easy to understand.

## Decision 7: Not Buying Banks — Ever

**Context:** Smith has stated repeatedly that he will never invest in bank stocks. He held this view even when banks were popular during the 2010-2015 recovery.

**Why:**
> "Banks are leveraged 20:1. A 5% loss on assets wipes out all equity. The downside is total loss. The upside is a 12% ROE. The math doesn't work."

**Was He Right?**
- UK bank shares have been poor long-term investments
- Barclays, Lloyds, RBS all trade at or below prices from 15+ years ago
- Regulatory costs increased significantly after 2008
- Bank ROEs remain structurally low (8–12%)

**Lesson:** Some industries are structurally uninvestable. No price is safe.

---

## Decision 8: Buying Meta During the Cambridge Analytica Scandal (2018)

**Context:** In 2018, Meta (then Facebook) was in crisis. The Cambridge Analytica scandal broke. The stock dropped ~40% from highs. Regulators threatened breakup. Users were outraged.

**Smith's Move:** He bought Meta shares heavily during the crisis.

**Why:**
- Meta had extraordinary fundamentals: ROCE > 30%, cash conversion > 90%, operating margins ~40%
- The scandal, while serious, did not threaten the core business model
- User engagement barely changed despite the outrage
- The moat (network effects, data, scale) was intact
- The price had become attractive

**Outcome:**
- Meta became one of Fundsmith's largest positions
- In 2024, Meta was the #1 contributor to Fundsmith's returns
- Smith's thesis was validated: scandal ≠ business deterioration

**Lesson:** Crisis creates opportunity when the core business is intact.

---

## Decision 9: Selling Apple After Zero Revenue Growth (2024)

**Context:** Fundsmith owned Apple for several years. In 2024, Smith sold the entire position.

**Why:**
> "Apple had two years of zero revenue growth and the share price doubled. That's multiple expansion, not business performance. We don't want to hold stocks where the valuation has run ahead of the business."

**The Math:**
- Apple's revenue was essentially flat for 2023 and 2024
- The share price doubled over the same period
- This means P/E multiples expanded significantly
- Smith does not want to own stocks where price gains come entirely from multiple expansion

**Outcome:**
- Smith sold at a good price
- Apple's subsequent performance is TBD
- The decision reflects Step 2 of Smith's framework: "Don't overpay"

---

## Decision 10: Selling Diageo Over GLP-1 Risk (2024)

**Context:** Diageo (Guinness, Johnnie Walker, Smirnoff) was a long-term Fundsmith holding. In 2024, Smith sold.

**Why:**
> "The emergence of GLP-1 weight loss drugs changes alcohol consumption patterns. This is a structural change, not a cyclical one."

**The Thesis:**
- GLP-1 drugs (Ozempic, Wegovy, Mounjaro) reduce appetite and cravings
- Early data suggests users drink less alcohol
- If GLP-1 adoption grows significantly, alcohol consumption declines structurally
- Diageo's brand moat cannot protect against lower demand

**Outcome:**
- Smith recognized a structural threat before it showed in the financials
- Similar to his Accounting for Growth approach: see what others miss

---

## Decision 11: Not Buying Nvidia (2023–2025)

**Context:** Nvidia was the hottest stock in the world. AI mania pushed valuations to extreme levels.

**Smith's Position:** He does not own Nvidia and likely never will.

**Why:**
1. **Valuation:** At 50+ times earnings, the stock is priced for perfection
2. **Cyclical hardware:** Nvidia sells chips. Chip companies are inherently cyclical, even if AI demand grows.
3. **Competition risk:** Large customers (Google, Amazon, Microsoft) are designing their own chips
4. **Technological uncertainty:** AI chip architecture could shift in ways Nvidia's dominance doesn't survive

**What Critics Say:**
> "Smith is missing the biggest technology shift since the internet. AI is real. Nvidia is the pick-and-shovel play."

**Smith's Response:**
> "Nvidia contributed 21% of the S&P 500's return last year. At 53x earnings, the valuation is absurd. That's not investing — that's a lottery ticket."

---

## Decision 12: Not Changing the Process After Underperformance (2022–2025)

**Context:** From 2022–2025, Fundsmith underperformed the market. Growth stocks, AI stocks, and value plays all outperformed quality investing.

**Smith's Response:** He did nothing. He changed nothing.

**Why:**
> "We underperformed the market significantly in 2024. We know this. We accept it. We will not change our process because of one bad year."

**The Logic:**
- Quality investing has historically gone through multi-year periods of underperformance
- The businesses in the portfolio are, on average, the highest quality in the world
- ROCE, margins, and cash conversion metrics remain strong
- Changing the process would mean abandoning the philosophy

**Outcome:** Still to be determined. If quality investing reverts to its historical mean, Smith will be vindicated. If the market has structurally shifted, he will not.

---

## Decision 13: Public Criticism of Unilever's Board (2025)

**Context:** Unilever replaced CEO Hein Schumacher after just 18 months. Schumacher had been refocusing Unilever on its best brands and dropping non-core initiatives.

**Smith's Response (at 2025 AGM):**
> "Hein Schumacher was the right CEO. He understood that Unilever needed to focus on its best brands and stop chasing social purpose. The board sacked him after 18 months. That's not long enough to turn around a company of that size. I'm disappointed."

**Why This Matters:**
Publicly criticizing a company's board is unusual for a fund manager. It can damage relationships. But Smith has always prioritized honesty over diplomacy.

---

## Decision 14: Remaining Independent (Ongoing)

**Context:** Fundsmith has received numerous offers from larger financial institutions to acquire the business.

**Smith's Position:** He has kept Fundsmith independent.

**Why:**
- Independence allows him to invest the way he wants
- No parent company pressure to generate short-term profits
- No conflicts of interest with a larger financial group
- He can keep fees low because there's no corporate parent demanding margin

---

## Summary: Decision Patterns

| Pattern | Examples |
|---------|----------|
| **Choose truth over career** | Accounting for Growth |
| **Go against the crowd** | No banks, no Nvidia, buy Meta during scandal |
| **Start late, succeed** | Fundsmith at 57 |
| **Simplify radically** | No performance fees, three-step framework |
| **Stay disciplined** | No process changes after 3 years of underperformance |
| **Sell when valuation exceeds business** | Apple 2024 |
| **Recognize structural threats early** | Diageo / GLP-1 |
| **Speak honestly, even when it costs** | Unilever criticism |
