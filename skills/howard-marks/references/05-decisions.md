# 05 — Key Decisions & Investment Cases

Howard Marks's most significant investment decisions and cases demonstrate how his philosophy translates into action.

---

## 1. Founding Oaktree Capital (1995)

**Context:** Marks spent 16 years at Citicorp and 10 years at TCW Group, building expertise in high-yield debt and distressed securities. In 1995, he and four partners (Bruce Karsh, Sheldon Stone, Richard Masson, and Larry Keele) left TCW to found Oaktree.

**Decision:** Leave a stable position at a major institution to start an independent asset manager focused on distressed debt.

**Why It Matters:**
- Marks had identified that distressed debt was an inefficient, under-covered market
- The team had domain expertise that was hard to replicate
- They backed their conviction with entrepreneurial risk
- Oaktree grew to $224 billion in assets under management

**Result:** One of the most successful independent asset managers in history. The "founder team" structure enabled focused, long-term thinking.

---

## 2. The 2000 Dot-Com Call

**Context:** By late 1999, technology stocks were in a frenzy. Marks had no special expertise in tech, but he observed behavior around him—people quitting jobs to trade stocks, cocktail party chatter about the market.

**Decision:** On January 1, 2000, he made a strong defensive pivot, reducing risk across Oaktree's portfolios. He didn't predict the crash—he sensed the psychology was extreme.

**Framework Applied:**
- Second-level thinking: "Everyone is euphoric. What does that mean for prices?"
- Pendulum positioning: The pendulum was at an extreme of optimism
- "Where we stand in the cycle": The credit cycle was ripe for a correction
- The difference between "good company" and "good investment" at high prices

**Result:** Oaktree avoided the 50-80% losses in tech and preserved capital through the crash.

**Marks's Reflection:** "This wasn't about knowing technology. It was about 'sensing the temperature'—observing people's behavior and recognizing excess."

## 3. The 2008 Financial Crisis: Raising $10.9 Billion

**Context:** In 2007, Marks wrote "It's All Good," a memo warning that optimism had reached dangerous levels. He saw red flags in the subprime mortgage market and structured credit.

**Decision:**
- **2007:** Raised caution flags, reduced risk exposure
- **2008:** When panic hit, Oaktree raised the largest distressed debt fund in history — $10.9 billion
- **2008-2009:** Aggressively bought distressed assets at "giveaway prices"

**Framework Applied:**
- Contrarianism: Buy when others are selling
- Cycle awareness: The pendulum had swung from euphoria to panic
- Risk assessment: Real risk had declined because prices were low
- Margin of safety: Assets were priced for disaster, not just for bad outcomes
- Asymmetric returns: Massive upside potential with limited downside

**Result:** The fund generated exceptional returns. Marks called this "the opportunity of a lifetime." Oaktree earned enormous profits for investors who had the courage to commit capital during the panic.

---

## 4. Avoiding Cryptocurrency (2017-Present)

**Context:** In 2017, cryptocurrencies, especially Bitcoin, experienced a massive bull run. Many institutions were exploring crypto exposure.

**Decision:** Marks wrote "I Beg to Differ" (2017), strongly advising investors to avoid cryptocurrencies. He argued they had no intrinsic value, no cash flows, and were driven purely by speculation.

**Framework Applied:**
- Intrinsic value principle: Assets without cash flows cannot be valued
- Second-level thinking: "Everyone is buying crypto. If the consensus is already long, who's left to buy?"
- Risk awareness: Speculative manias destroy capital

**Result:** Crypto went through multiple boom-bust cycles. While Bitcoin continued to appreciate long-term, many crypto projects failed. Marks's view on gold and crypto being "anchorless" assets remained unchanged as of 2026.

---

## 5. The 2023 "Sea Change" Memo

**Context:** After a decade of near-zero interest rates (2010-2021), the Federal Reserve raised rates aggressively in 2022-2023.

**Decision:** Marks wrote "Sea Change" (2023), arguing that the multi-decade decline in interest rates had ended. This represented a structural shift in the investing environment.

**Implications He Identified:**
- Fixed income would become attractive again (after years of being "dead")
- The easy money era of leverage-driven returns was over
- Investors needed to adjust return expectations downward
- Active management would matter more in a higher-rate world

**Framework Applied:**
- Cycle recognition: The credit cycle had changed regime
- Long-term thinking: A structural shift, not a tactical one
- Honesty: Acknowledging that the old playbook needed updating

**Result:** The "Sea Change" framing became widely discussed in institutional investing circles.

---

## 6. Distressed Debt Investing During COVID-19 (2020)

**Context:** March 2020 saw the fastest bear market in history as COVID-19 caused a global economic shutdown. Credit markets froze.

**Decision:** Oaktree raised significant capital to deploy into distressed debt. Marks wrote "The Value of Uncertainty" and "Calibrating" to guide investors through the crisis.

**Framework Applied:**
- Crisis buying: "The biggest opportunities come when fear peaks"
- Cycle awareness: This was a classic panic-driven sell-off
- Contrarianism: Everyone wanted cash; Oaktree wanted to deploy it
- Price/value relationship: Assets were at massive discounts

**Result:** Oaktree generated 20%+ returns from the COVID dislocations across its distressed debt funds. However, the recovery was far faster than 2008 — the Fed's unprecedented intervention compressed the opportunity window from years to months. Marks noted this as evidence that central bank intervention was reducing the amplitude of market cycles.

---

## 7. The Persistent Call: "Don't Bet Against China" (2025)

**Context:** Amid US-China tensions and concerns about China's economic slowdown.

**Decision:** Marks publicly stated "Don't bet China losing" in mid-2025, echoing Buffett's "don't bet against America."

**Framework Applied:**
- Long-term perspective: Short-term pessimism creates long-term opportunity
- Contrarianism: When the crowd is bearish on China, that might be the wrong side
- Acknowledging complexity: He didn't predict China's outcomes, just argued against betting against it

## Investment Style Summary

| Dimension | Marks's Approach |
|-----------|-----------------|
| **Primary Asset Class** | Credit / distressed debt |
| **Position Sizing** | Concentrated in high-conviction situations but diversified at portfolio level |
| **Holding Period** | Long-term (years to decades in credit investments) |
| **Decision Frequency** | Low — "only 5 great market calls in 25 years" |
| **Risk Management** | Avoid permanent loss of capital above all else |
| **Leverage** | Minimal to none |
| **Information Edge** | Deep industry relationships, distressed debt expertise |
| **Psychological Edge** | Emotional stability, contrarian patience |
