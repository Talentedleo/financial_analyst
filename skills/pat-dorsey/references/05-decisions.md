# Pat Dorsey — Analytical Decisions & Case Studies

An analysis of key decisions and analytical cases from Pat Dorsey's framework, Morningstar's moat ratings, and his portfolio management at Dorsey Asset Management.

---

> **Note on sourcing:** Pat Dorsey himself does not publish case-by-case decision rationales. The analysis below is reconstructed from his published framework (The Little Book That Builds Wealth), Morningstar's moat rating methodology, and observable portfolio activity from 13F filings.

---

## 1. Core Analytical Framework (Not a Single Decision, But a System)

Dorsey's most important "decision" was the creation of the **systematic moat identification process** itself. Rather than individual stock picks, this system is his lasting contribution.

### The Moat Identification Checklist

| Step | Question | What to Look For |
|------|----------|-----------------|
| 1 | Is there a real moat? | Test each of the five sources |
| 2 | Is it a mistaken moat? | Check against the four traps |
| 3 | How wide is the moat? | Wide (20+ yrs) vs Narrow (10+ yrs) |
| 4 | What is the moat trend? | Positive, Stable, or Negative |
| 5 | What could erode it? | Tech change, regulation, competition |
| 6 | Does management protect or destroy it? | Capital allocation track record |
| 7 | What is the right price? | Valuation within margin of safety |

## 2. Selected Moat Rating Examples (From Dorsey's Book)

These examples come from *The Little Book That Builds Wealth* and illustrate how Dorsey applies his framework:

### Wide Moat Examples (from the book)

| Company | Moat Source | Rationale |
|---------|-------------|-----------|
| **Coca-Cola** | Brand (Intangible Asset) | Pricing power; customers pay premium; global distribution network creates scale advantage |
| **Microsoft** | Switching Costs + Network Effects | Office/Windows deeply integrated into business operations; high retraining/swap costs |
| **Visa** | Network Effects | Two-sided network; more merchants → more users → more merchants; closed network advantage |
| **3M** | Patents (Intangible Assets) | Broad patent portfolio; demonstrated track record of continuous innovation |
| **Walmart** | Cost Advantage (Scale) | Massive distribution network; economies of scale that competitors cannot replicate |
| **Moody's** | Regulatory License (Intangible Asset) | Regulated industry (bond ratings) with pricing freedom; regulatory barrier to entry |

### Mistaken Moat Examples (What Looks Like a Moat But Isn't)

| Company | Mistaken Moat Trap | Why It's Not a Real Moat |
|---------|-------------------|--------------------------|
| **Dell (historical)** | Process-based cost advantage | Process advantages get copied; Dell's direct model was eventually replicated |
| **Best Buy** | Great execution | Retail execution is not structural; competition (Amazon) exposed the lack of moat |
| **Starbucks** | Great product + brand | Brand only creates a moat if it leads to pricing power or customer captivity — Starbucks has some brand moat but faces low switching costs |
| **Tommy Hilfiger** | Strong market share | Fashion brands are fickle; market share can disappear with changing consumer preferences |

## 3. Moat Erosion Cases (from the book)

Dorsey uses these examples to show how even strong moats can break:

| Company | Original Moat | Erosion Cause |
|---------|---------------|---------------|
| **Kodak** | Patents + Brand | Technological obsolescence (digital photography) |
| **Newspapers** | Local monopoly + Switching costs | Internet disrupted classifieds and local advertising |
| **AT&T / Landlines** | Regulatory license + Network effects | Technology shift to mobile + VOIP |
| **Microsoft (Zune)** | — | Corporate empire building — failed diversification attempt |

### Portfolio Characteristics
- **Concentrated:** Very few holdings for a fund managing ~$750M
- **Low turnover:** Positions held for years, not quarters
- **Quality bias:** All holdings exhibit clear moat characteristics
- **No trading around earnings:** Portfolio changes appear to reflect fundamental thesis changes, not short-term sentiment

### What the Portfolio Tells Us About Dorsey's Recent Thinking
- Moat analysis still drives all decisions
- Technology companies with network effects remain preferred
- Old-economy moats (brands, regulatory licenses) are complemented by tech exposure
- Conviction is high — concentrated positioning means each position is thoroughly researched

## 5. Industry-Level Moat Assessments (From the Book)

Dorsey categorizes industries by their likelihood of harboring moated companies:

| Industry | Moat Potential | Key Moat Type | Reason |
|----------|---------------|---------------|--------|
| Business Services | High | Switching Costs | Tight integration with customers |
| Financials | High | Network Effects / Intangibles | Sticky assets, exchange network effects |
| Healthcare (Pharma/Devices) | Medium-High | Patents (Intangibles) | Unique drugs/devices dominate niches |
| Software | High | Switching Costs | Integration with business operations |
| Media | Medium | Scale / Distribution | Distribution networks (but tech disruption risk) |
| Energy (Pipelines) | Medium | Regulatory / Scale | Regulation without price control |
| Consumer Goods | Low-Medium | Brand (Intangibles) | Some strong brands, but many fad-driven |
| Technology Hardware | Low | None | Standardized, commoditized |
| Consumer Services | Very Low | None | Price-conscious, many substitutes |
| Industrial Materials | Very Low | None (except niche) | Highly commoditized |

## 6. Selling Decisions Framework

Dorsey's four reasons to sell (from *The Little Book That Builds Wealth*):

1. **Mistake:** The original investment thesis was wrong; you misidentified the moat or overestimated its durability
2. **Change:** The facts have changed; the moat is eroding or the business fundamentals have permanently deteriorated
3. **Better Alternative:** A clearly superior opportunity exists; relative value comparison
4. **Risk Management:** The position has grown too large relative to portfolio; concentration risk
